## Time as a relevant dimension
Any discussion about sustainability, efficiency, circularity etc is a question about how something is affected by the passage of time. In other words, the central question is how some quantity or quality changes *across* time. That might be a change in temperature, enthalpy, biomass, self-esteem, reputation, money, usable plastic, paper, or any other quantity that is changing over time.

In addition time is the dimension emergence happens in. This might be individual beings being born or dying, it might be entire species / categories dying or emerging. Complex adaptive systems vs. complex maladaptive systems can only be distinguished across time, because is constitutive. Just as in relativity, time is seldom reducible to a simple scalar.

The central question being considered when something changes across time is: what is the *typical* outcome? 

In almost all cases the typical outcome is at best hard to calculate, and all too often impossible to calculate. 

**There are three domains to consider:**
1. there is no stochasticity whatsoever, including no surprises or swan events ever (impossible in reality, given that the universe began with a big bang, the biggest swan event ever, and may well end in a big crunch); 
2. there is stochasticity, but over long enough time the typical outcome of the impact of the stochasticity trends towards the average outcome, i.e., the expectation value; 
3. there is stochasticity, and the typical outcome of the impact of the stochasticity does not trend towards the average outcome (expectation value).

Domain 3 must be used unless there is solid evidence that either 1 (no stachasticity at all) holds true mathematically, or at the very least that 2 is either mathematically true, or at least that the difference between the typical outcome and the expected outcome is negligible versus the conclusions you're trying to draw. 

Almost everything across neoclassical and heterodox economics assumes that the dynamics are in domain 2. But without being being aware that this simplifying assumption is being made, without quantifying the error introduced, nor proving it to be the case.

Domain 3 **must** be used if any of the following are true:
1. the stochastic dynamics has a multiplicative component (e.g. biomass growth, compound interest, revenue doubling each year); 
2. losses eventually hit an absorbing boundary, (e.g. a company goes insolvent, the last remaining breeding pair of a specie gets eaten); or if there is a generative boundary, i.e., new entities / beings get born, entirely new classes of entities / species can be created. 
3. growth eventually hits an absorbing boundary due to resource limits (e.g. a specie eats the last of its prey and then also dies, or an economy overshoots its planetary boundaries); or an entirely new class of resources enters the picture. 

## The ergodicity misclassification

Economics has a long tradition of drawing from physics. However there are critical simplifications that were made that need to be upgraded for economics to become a scientific discipline.

In chapter 4, the section *The misclassification of economics* in *Money - Sustainability: The Missing Link* by Bernard Lietaer, Christian Arnsperger, Sally Goerner, Stefan Brunnhuber, March 2012 (report from the Club of Rome - EU chapter to Finance Watch and the World Business Academy), two absolutely critical misclassifications are described.
1. Lack of awareness in economics of the difference between closed and open systems, leading to treating all as closed systems.
2. Neglecting the second law of thermodynamics, that entropy is always increasing.

A third critical misclassification was first suspected in economics just two decades ago, and the first significant publication on just how grave this misclassification is in economics was first published in 2016 by Ole Peters and Murray Gell-Mann, and thereby founding *Ergodicity Economics* ([Evaluating gambles using dynamics](https://doi.org/10.1063/1.4940236)" published in the journal _Chaos_ in February 2016).  

They showed very clearly that aspects of neoclassical and heterodox economic theory were aftermarket patches repairing the consequences of this third misclassification. For example, much of utility theory, as shown in the original Peters and Gell-Mann paper. 

In the decade since, ever more aspects, puzzling to neoclassical economics, have been shown to be explained when the correct equations are used. For example, over the past decade Ergodicity Economics presents an ever growing body of evidence for the following. 
- Rational humans act to optimize their wealth *across* time, and not maximize an expected utility average across parallel worlds. [paper1](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/annals-of-actuarial-science/article/insurance-as-an-ergodicity-problem/193215EAD7D1731B81EB8A61C99804D0) and [paper 2](https://www.santafe.edu/news-center/news/peters-and-gell-mann-author-most-read-chaos-paper-2016). 
- This in many ways shows that the explanations of "irrational humans" are needless patches hiding the original misclassification of Domain 3 dynamics as Domain 1 or 2. 
- Loss avoidance is not a cognitive bias, but rather the rational response to domain 3 dynamics. 

There are parallels to physics 150 years ago. The gap between the predictions of statistical mechanics on the one hand, and thermodynamics on the other, had puzzled physicists. Until they realised that almost all physical processes are actually in domain 3, and they need to use the appropriate equation: the time average, or typical outcome *across* time is what matters, not the ensemble average *in* time. 

In closing, the three misclassifications are related. Only if a system is closed can the dynamics have any chance of being ergodic (domain 1 or 2); and the second law in a given system is a consequence it having ergodic or non-ergodic dynamics. 

## The correct domain 3 equations
For some time dependent variable $x(t)$ (for example, the amount of some capital) that can have some value out of a set of all possible values for $x$. This set of all possible values is called the ensemble. 

The typical outcome across time, also called the time average, is obtained by looking at how the quantity changes across time across time, from the start $t=0$  to the end $t=T$.
$$
        \bar{x}_T = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T} x(t)
$$
where each of the $x(t)$ are taken from the ensemble with $N$ possible values for $x(t)$.

This, the typical outcome, is always the correct number to look at if you want to draw conclusions about sustainability. 

Or, in fact, about anything in the economy, in society, or about the developmental progress of an individual through their life. (For the first paper bringing the insights of ergodicity into psychology see Molenaar, 2007, *A Manifesto on Psychology as Idiographic Science: Bringing the Person Back Into Scientific Psychology, This Time Forever*.) 

However, the time average, in most realistic cases, cannot be calculated, but only simulated. And in some cases not even simulated. 

There is another kind of average, called the expected value $\langle x \rangle$, a.k.a. the ensemble average, often just the average, which can be calculated very easily. It is the arithmetic average over the ensemble *in* time. 
$$
        \langle x  \rangle  = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} x_i
$$
for an ensemble containing $N$ possible values for $x$. For continuous  $x(t)$ replace the sum with an integral over all  $x(t)$.

If, and only if, the dynamics of the system are in domain 1 or 2 are the two equal. (Strictly speaking, only when ${T \to \infty}$.) 
$$
        \langle x  \rangle  = \bar{x}_{T \to \infty}
$$
Only if they are strictly equal for infinite time, or if at least the deficit is negligible vs. the conclusions being drawn, can you use the easily calculated expectation value to estimate, or substitute for, the number you need, the time average or typical outcome.   

This was the problem in physics that led Ludwig Blotzmann in 1870-1880 to propose (with success) that using the time average in statistical physics would lead to the same predictions thermodynamics was giving. This was refined and formalised in physics and mathematics over the next 50 years, with the key proof by the brilliant mathematician John von Neumann in 1932 of the *Mean Ergodic Theorem*. 

But by then Léon Walras’ *Éléments d’économie pure* (1874) had reified into economics the mathematics from old physics, carrying with it the ergodic misclassification.


## Implications for practice and theory

The key implication is that we must re-examine the arguments behind much of the prevailing wisdom on why we have negative externalities, on why businesses and their leaders behave as they do, on why we have such a gap between ultra-rich and poor, why the middle income is being hollowed out, on why some people win the nobel prize but others, perhaps more deserving, do not. 

For example, [Peters and Adamou](https://royalsocietypublishing.org/rsta/article/380/2227/20200425/112185/The-ergodicity-solution-of-the-cooperation) have shown that when cooperation vs. competition is looked at using the correct domain 3 equation, i.e., which strategy maximises an individual's typical outcome across time, a blend of cooperation and competition emerges as the rational self-interested strategy. No need for any values-based explanation! 

### Energy Network Theory

I've followed this work ever since first meeting Bernard Lietaer when I was living in Brussels 2007-2010, and having conversations with him in his apartment, where I first encountered the first aspects of this work. It made intuitive sense to me.

The more I read the emerging papers of ergodicity ecnomics; and especially writing my DIY handbook to building a new economic paradigm grounded in Econophysics (Rebuild the Economy, Leadership, and You) and the subsequent deeper dive into the implications for regenerative investing and entrepreneurship (The Ergodic Investor and Entrepreneur); the more clear it became that ergodicity economics was at least part of what would ground some of the hypotheses in this research. 

This begins with recognising that the foundational equations of energy networks, unless proven to be in domains 1 or 2, must be treated in domain 3. In other words, explicitly including time, the hypothesis is that the foundational equations become, when looking for the typical outcome over some time $T$, something like:
$$
\begin{align}
H(T) &= -\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T}\sum_{i(t)=1}^{n(t)} p_i(t) \log\left(p_i(t)\right) \\
C(T) &= -\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T}\sum_{i(t),j(t)=1}^{n(t)} p_{ij}(t) \log(p_{ij}(t)) \\
A(T) &=  \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T}\sum_{i(t),j(t)=1}^{n(t)} p_{ij}(t) \log\left(\frac{p_{ij}(t)}{p_i(t)p_j(t)}\right) \\
\Phi(T) &=  -\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T}\sum_{i(t),j(t)=1}^{n(t)} p_{ij}(t) \log\left(\frac{p_{ij}(t)}{p_i(t)p_j(t)}\right)
\end{align}
$$
(I've not worked through all the details and implications; that deserves a publication or two just on this, for example Robert's Ecoduality paper may yield new insights building in time dependence.)

Or looking at the equivalent equations for energy network analysis the equations for the typical outcome across time, i.e., the time average, may be:
$$
\begin{align}
C(T) &= -\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T}\sum_{i(t),j(t)=1}^{n(t)} M_{ij}(t) \log\left(M_{ij}(t)\right) \\
A(T) &=  \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T}\sum_{i(t),j(t)=1}^{n(t)} M_{ij}(t) \log\left(\frac{M_{ij}(t)M_{..}(t)}{M_i(t)M_j(t)}\right) \\
\Phi(T) &=  -\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T}\sum_{i(t),j(t)=1}^{n(t)} M_{ij}(t) \log\left(\frac{M_{ij}(t)}{M_i(t)M_j(t)}\right) \\
\end{align}
$$
where $M_{ij}$ is used instead of the original $T_{ij}$ to avoid confusion with time. This means that, instead of using the average flow between nodes $i$ and $j$ over some time period, say a year, we look in detail at the flow in each time step between $t=0$ and $t=T$. By simulating once the network across time with the appropriate random number generators for each node and connection, as well as appropriate random number generations for nodes ceasing to exist, or coming into existence, as well as for entire classes of nodes emerging or dying, we create one single path that the network could follow across time. 

If, for  ${T \to \infty}$, each quantity tends to the original time-invariant number, you have ergodic dynamics. 

If not, we can repeat this for a sufficiently large number of paths to estimate the median outcome at some characteristic time; the spread of typical outcomes; and from that estimate the degree of non-ergodicity for that quantity over some characteristic time $\tau$.

Consider, for example, Fig. 6 of the paper *Measuring regenerative economics: 10 principles and measures undergirding systemic economic health* (Fath et al. 2019). The central area (organized complexity) and the right area (statistics) have, in almost all relevant cases in an economy, non-ergodic dynamics.  The insights from Ergodicity Economics; that collaboration, profit pooling between competing business, and much more; arise as the rational choice to optimise the time average suggests that, of the REP (Regenerative Economy Principles), at least REP1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9 may well be at least partially, and in some cases totally a consequence of living beings optimising in domain 3. REP 10 is then the obvious enabler of that. 

For an example of the implications of non-ergodicity in physics, the recent paper of Bossen and Mauro [*Frozen in time: A review of non-ergodic physical systems*](https://ceramics.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/epdf/10.1111/jace.20092) deals with various examples using energy landscape theory. Which will hint at the work needed to explicitly deal with non-ergodic dynamics in Ecological / Energy Network Analysis (ENA). 

Looking at the early paper *Quantifying sustainability: Resilience, efficiency and the return of information theory* of Robert E. Ulanowicz, Sally J. Goerner, Bernard Lietaer, Rocio Gomez through the lens of ergodicity economics we propose that a number of the hypotheses in that paper can be grounded through ergodicity theory. 

For example, the discussion in section 5 on the most appropriate metric for robustness, where first $A\Phi$ is considered (which peaks at $A=\Phi=0.5$), but as you rightly point out no reason is known for why that must be the case. Then $-\alpha \ln \alpha$ is proposed, but again no reason is known for why the peak must be at $\alpha = 1/e$. And so you propose  $-\alpha^\beta \ln \alpha^\beta$  where $\beta$ is a parameter determining the optimum for a given system. As you then write:  *There is no a priori reason to assume that the value of $\beta$ is universal. There might be one value of b most germane to ecosystem networks, another for economic communities, and still another for networks of genetic switching.* 

In this, $\beta$ is then determined by fitting the curve to data from various systems. In this paper, and others, a range of different $\beta$ and peak $\alpha$ are obtained from the data. Our proposal, which requires significant further work, is that by re-examining the research now using only domain 3, non-ergodic dynamics, unless it can be proven to be domain 1 or 2 (ergodic, or insignificantly non-ergodic) will make clear that $\beta$ is a time-dependent function of the degree of non-ergodicity, quite likely with some number other factors. 

Note also that there are two classes of non-ergodic dynamics: 
- weak ergodicity breaking with a smaller degree of non-ergodicity (e.g. multiplicative growth, fixed ensemble); and 
- strong ergodicity breaking (the ensemble itself changes) with a significantly larger degree of non-ergodicity. 
Nature, and our economy, is observed to have punctuated equilibrium. For example, Figures 1, 2, 3 of Fath, B. D., Dean, C. A., & Katzmair, H. (2015),  *Navigating the adaptive cycle: An approach to managing the resilience of social systems. Ecology and Society*, would, I suggest, gain grounding when time is included explicitly. 

During lower degree of non-ergodicity (i.e., stable business category, weak ergodicity breaking) the optimum of $A$ vs $\Phi$ (peak in the curve $-\alpha^\beta \ln \alpha^\beta$ is closer to the efficiency limit. But then, when an entire business concept is replaced by a new one (e.g. manual coding to vibe coding) the peak in the curve moves away from efficiency towards the $\Phi$, resilience limit. Of course, it is only there for some period of time, until the new business concept becomes sufficiently clear to know what the new efficiency-resilience frame of reference is. 

This is a crucial emergent property when looking at a complex (mal)adaptive system over time, because efficiency can only be measured or navigated towards against some frame of reference (FoR). Strong ergodicity breaking will most likely shift the former FoR to some new and quite different (qualitative and quantitatively) FoR. And while that shift is happening the FoR is most likely to be undefinable. 

In practice for our human economy, high levels of diversity mean that there is more chance that the economy will have at least one company that is, by accident, right in the new FoR once it becomes visible. 

For example, if a system is in domain 1, i.e., it is fully ergodic over some relevant time period, (for example, a modern precision manufactured petrol engine) then the peak $\alpha$ will be close to the efficiency limit. Nothing much is changing, the FoR is fixed and clearly known, etc. Both for the design of the engines, but also for the operations of the automobile sector companies. 

But now that electric cars are growing rapidly, fossil fuel supply changing, electricity generation changing, early autonomous vehicles are on the roads, the entire FoR is changing and in fact is currently undefined. So now both the optimum $\alpha$ for propulsion units themselves shows a shift to higher diversity and connectivity, as well as the players in the automobile sector. New companies like Tesla with no prior experience of automobile manufacture have become dominant players. 


## Deconflation: different related terminology

Ergodic is not the same as predictable from a probability distribution, and non-ergodic is not the same as unpredictable! Some non-ergodic systems have typical outcomes just as predictable as ergodic ones. The key point is that you must make the prediction using the time average not the ensemble average. 

Another terminology related to this is made with the distinction between risk and uncertainty (e.g. by the late economist Paul Davidson in [Is Probability Theory Relevant for Uncertainty? A Post Keynesian Perspective](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/4730787_Is_Probability_Theory_Relevant_for_Uncertainty_A_Post_Keynesian_Perspective).  Risk is defined as "described by some ensemble of probability ratios" and uncertainty as events that cannot have probability ratios assigned to them. Whilst ergodic vs. non-ergodic has been used for this, not everything called non-ergodic in this arena is truly mathematically non-ergodic. 

First of all, note that even a truly ergodic system can spend significant amounts of time far from the mean. This means that, even for a company in a truly ergodic system, a short history of performance cannot be used to determine the true probability distribution, and hence predict the future.  This short-term divergence can look like non-ergodicity, even though it is not. 

Then comes Keynesian uncertainty, defined as inherently unknowable events, which is strong ergodicity breaking.
